# FTM and BTM Energy Storage Reports: Summary and Interview Questions

Sources (your uploaded files):
- **FTM report**: "India Stationary Energy Storage Market Overview, Part I: Front-of-the-Meter, 2023-2032", 10th edition, by Customized Energy Solutions (CES) with IESA. Data and tenders up to about Sept 2024.
- **BTM report**: "India Stationary Energy Storage Market Overview, Part II: Behind-the-Meter and Railways, 2023-2032", 9th edition, same publishers. Data for 2023.

**Both reports are dated** (2024 and 2023 data). Today is Nov 2026, so say "as per the 2024 CES/IESA report" in the interview and mention that newer tenders and policies have moved on (for example MP's 2026 BESS tenders).

---

## PART A: What the FTM report says

### 1. Scope and market size
- Covers two FTM segments: **utility-scale projects** and **discom-side storage**.
- **Installed (2024):** about **220 MWh of BESS** and **4.7 GW of pumped hydro (PHES)**. About **2.04 GWh of standalone BESS** was awarded in tenders in 2023-24.
- **Forecast (cumulative 2023-2032):**
  | Case | Total | Utility-scale | Discom-side |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Worst | ~107 GWh | ~103.5 GWh | ~4.3 GWh |
  | Base | ~173 GWh | ~164.7 GWh | ~8.5 GWh |
  | Best | ~255 GWh | ~236.6 GWh | ~18.7 GWh |
  Utility-scale is **93 to 96%** of the market because projects are huge (hundreds to thousands of MWh). Discom-side is 4 to 7%.
- **CAGR 2024-32:** worst 51%, base 55%, best 53% (as printed in the report).
- Cases: **worst** = delays in PPAs and weak policy (about 40% of the NEP BESS target), **base** = business as usual (about 70%), **best** = full NEP target and pending policies approved.

### 2. Why India needs storage
- Smooth solar/wind output, give round-the-clock power, support morning and evening peaks, give ramping support.
- **National Electricity Plan (CEA, 2023):** BESS need of **8.68 GW / 34.72 GWh by 2026-27** and **47.24 GW / 236.22 GWh by 2031-32**, plus **26.68 GW of pumped storage** (PSP). High-demand case (+5%): 22.82 GW / 91.29 GWh by 2026-27 and 66.78 GW / 333.91 GWh by 2031-32.
- Renewable share of installed capacity was about **24%** and is expected to reach about **51% by 2030**.

### 3. Policy timeline (main milestones)
- 2021-22: ACC battery PLI (50 GWh target), CERC Ancillary Services Regulations include storage, MoP BESS competitive bidding guidelines, storage classified as part of the power system under the Electricity Act.
- 2023: VGF for **4,000 MWh** of BESS, MoP guidelines for pumped storage, **FDRE guidelines (June 2023)**, **CEA Resource Adequacy Framework (June 2023)**, **National Framework for Promoting ESS (Aug 2023)**, IEGC 2023 (storage in ancillary services, primary reserve).
- 2024: CEA distribution planning criteria, VGF operational guidelines (**up to 40% of capital cost**, commissioning within 24 months).
- **Gaps the report lists:** no storage-specific policy, no clear storage definition, weak ToD framework in many states, delayed DSM penalty implementation, no standard battery reuse/recycling framework, storage not yet in the green term-ahead market.

### 4. Obligations (ESO)
- Storage Obligation on an **energy basis**: **1.0% (2023-24), 1.5%, 2.0%, 2.5%, 3.0%, 3.5%, 4.0% (2029-30)**.
- Counts as met only if **at least 85% of the energy stored in the ESS comes from renewable sources**.
- Total RPO trajectory in the report goes from about 24.6% (2022-23) to 43.3% (2029-30).
- **National Framework (Aug 2023):** ESS equal to **5% of RE capacity** for RE projects above 5 MW (as printed in the report).

### 5. State policies
- State comparison table covers AP, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Telangana, TN, UP, Chhattisgarh, **MP**, Bihar, Haryana, Odisha.
- **MP in the table:** has a storage target, special tariff/bidding category, incentives, tax/duty exemptions, R&D support and ESO. **No** exemption from transmission charges and **no** must-run status.
- **MP Solar Energy Policy 2022:** aim of **50% RE capacity mix by 2030**; extra incentive if an RE project of X MW has at least **X/10 MWh storage** and maintains **CUF of at least 35%**; **100% electricity duty exemption for 10 years**, **15% stamp duty reimbursement** on private land, concessional government land, single-window clearance.
- **MP Pumped Hydro Scheme (April 2023):** promotes off-stream PSPs; MP floated a **13 GW** PSP tender after this, which was **on hold** at the time of the report.
- **MPERC Resource Adequacy Framework Regulations (March 2024):** state-level capacity planning for discoms.
- Other states: Rajasthan (storage X/2 MWh for hybrid projects, 25% transmission and wheeling charges for 7 years), UP (4-hour storage requirement and capital subsidy of ₹2.5 crore/MW for solar with 4-hour, 5 MW storage), Odisha, Maharashtra (50 MW storage target), West Bengal (first state with ancillary services regulations, Dec 2023), Telangana (EV and ESS manufacturing policy).

### 6. Renewable base
- Solar: **73 GW (2023) to 85.47 GW (June 2024)**. **MP about 4.86 GW**. Solar forecast 2032: base 440 GW, best 550 GW (NEP number).
- Wind: **45 GW to 46.65 GW**. MP about 6% of wind.
- Rajasthan case study: needs **up to 31 GWh of storage** by 2030 for its RE targets.

### 7. Pumped hydro (PHES)
- Pipeline is huge but slow. Of about 65 GW of identified projects, only about **3.3 GW operational**, 2.78 GW under construction, most (about 53.8 GW) only under investigation.
- Open-loop vs closed-loop; off-river projects are faster. 82 abandoned mines identified for reservoirs.
- **MP:** Greenko's **Gandhi Sagar (Neemuch) 1,440 MW**, being expanded to **1,920 MW**, about **11 GWh** of daily storage; MPPMCL **500 MW PSP tender** (Oct 2023, 40-year period, **no updates** at report time).
- NEP: **18.8 GW PSP and 51.5 GW BESS by 2032** (as quoted in the PSP tender slide).

### 8. Tenders and prices (2023-24 results)
- **SECI peak power (Apr 2023):** ₹4.64 to 4.73/kWh.
- **SJVN FDRE (Nov 2023):** about ₹4.38 to 4.39/kWh.
- **NHPC FDRE (Feb 2024):** ₹4.55 to 4.64/kWh.
- **SECI FDRE (Mar 2024):** ₹5.59 to 5.60/kWh; the supply was meant for **PSPCL (Punjab) and MPPMCL (MP)**. Only 480 MW of 1,500 MW was awarded.
- **NTPC FDRE (Mar 2024):** ₹4.64 to 4.72/kWh.
- **SECI peak power (Jul 2024):** about **₹3.41 to 3.42/kWh**.
- **SECI FDRE (Jul 2024):** about ₹4.98 to 4.99/kWh. **NHPC FDRE (Sep 2024):** about ₹4.37 to 4.38/kWh.
- **Standalone BESS (capacity payment):** GUVNL 250 MW/500 MWh at about **₹4.49 lakh/MW/month**, then GUVNL 500 MW/1000 MWh at about **₹3.72 lakh/MW/month**; SECI 1000 MW/2000 MWh (JSW and Reliance Power, 500 MW each); BRPL (Delhi) 20 MW/40 MWh at ₹57.59 lakh/MW/year.
- **Standalone tender summary:** about 4,000 MW in RFPs, about 2,500 MW awarded, about 1,000 MW cancelled. Key winners: JSW, IndiGrid, Reliance Power, Gensol.
- **FDRE winners by capacity:** ACME, ReNew, Juniper Green, Hero Future, Tata Power, Serentica among the largest.
- **Lesson:** in FDRE the tariff is sensitive to storage size, round-trip efficiency and auxiliary consumption; developers do not have to disclose planned storage size.
- **LCOS chart:** winning standalone bids were around ₹3.7 per kWh levelised.
- **Blended cost (RE + storage):** model optimised to deliver at least 50% of installed capacity for 6 lean hours a day; more storage hours raises tariff.

### 9. Case studies
- **Tata Power, Chhattisgarh (Rajnandgaon):** **100 MW solar + 40 MW / 120 MWh BESS**, for CSPDCL, commissioned March 2024; largest solar-plus-BESS project in India at the time.
- **Lakshadweep:** 1.7 MW solar + 1.4 MWh BESS, replaces diesel.
- **Global:** Edwards Sanborn (US) 1,300 MW / 3,287 MWh; Datang Hubei (China) 100 MWh **sodium-ion**.

### 10. Ancillary services
- Types: frequency control (primary, secondary, tertiary), voltage control, emergency (black start).
- **CERC Ancillary Services Regulations 2022:** PRAS, SRAS, TRAS plus voltage control and black start. NLDC (Grid-India) is the nodal agency; payments through the Regional Deviation Pool account; TRAS effective April 2023.
- **SECI/JSW pilot at Fatehgarh-III (Rajasthan):** **500 MW / 1000 MWh** BESS at **₹10.835 lakh/MW/month**. Split: **150 MW / 300 MWh for grid ancillary services (NLDC)**, **150 MW for the discom**, **200 MW merchant**. Conditions: **85% minimum round-trip efficiency, maximum two discharge cycles/day, 2.5% yearly degradation, 12-year life**. No inter-state transmission charges or losses on the ancillary portion.
- CERC approved a Fast Response Ancillary Services pilot with hydro in 2018.
- Report's view: most storage demand will come from **secondary** reserve; separate storage purely for FRAS is unlikely; DSM savings and HP-DAM (high price day-ahead market) add revenue.

### 11. Discom-side storage
- Value streams: peak shaving, time shifting, DSM penalty saving, power quality, frequency regulation, grid upgrade deferral (best in metros).
- Projects are small (hundreds of kWh to a few MWh): BRPL (40 MWh Kilokari, funded by GEAPP), TPDDL 10 MWh, KSEB 6 MWh, DHBVN 10 MWh, TANGEDCO 3 MWh. Several were **cancelled or on hold** (BYPL, UPPCL).
- DERC approved a BESS agreement with a **single-part tariff**.

### 12. Value chain and stakeholders
- Developers (ACME, Juniper, Tata Power, ReNew, Gensol, Serentica), BESS providers, cell suppliers (imported), PCS suppliers. LFP pack price trend is in the BTM report.

## PART B: What the BTM report says

### 1. Market size and trend
- **BTM stationary storage demand: 33 GWh in 2023**, up from 31 GWh in 2022. Base case **about 44 GWh by 2032 (CAGR 3%)**; best 4%, worst 1%.
- **Segments in 2023 (base, MWh):** Inverter backup about **20,300**, UPS **5,000**, Railways **2,700**, Telecom towers **2,400**, Rooftop solar **2,100**, Decentralized (microgrids and street lights) **105**.
- **2032 base:** Inverters 16,400 (shrinking), UPS 10,300, Telecom 7,500, Railways 5,100, Rooftop solar 4,200, Decentralized 62.
- Demand is mainly driven by **power reliability problems**; as the grid improves, inverters and microgrids shrink while telecom, data centres (UPS), rooftop solar and railways grow.

### 2. Chemistry
- **Lead acid still dominates**, more than 98% in all segments except telecom.
- **Li-ion penetration 2023:** telecom **67%**, UPS about 3%, rooftop solar about 1%, inverters about 0.1%. By 2032 (base): telecom 93%, UPS 47%, rooftop solar 32%, inverters 10%.
- **Prices:** LFP pack about **$156/kWh (2023)**, falling to about **$94/kWh by 2032**; NMC from about $200 to $120. Cells are about **70% of pack cost** and are all imported (China, South Korea); BMS and housing are made locally.
- **GST:** lead-acid 28%, lithium-ion 18%. Rooftop solar plus battery combined 12%.
- Domestic push: ACC PLI (the BTM report mentions **30 GWh** allocated to Reliance, Ola Electric, Rajesh Exports; the FTM report mentions a 50 GWh programme target and a later 10 GWh bid round). Also Exide, Amara Raja, Tata, Suzuki, Livguard planning plants (about 95 GWh total ambitions).

### 3. Rooftop solar (RTS)
- Cumulative **9 GW (end 2022) to over 11 GW (end 2023)**; 1.7 GW added in 2023. Residential had the fastest growth; C&I is over 75% of capacity.
- Forecast: base **59 GW by 2032**, best **100 GW** (PM Surya Ghar / Suryodaya, 1 crore homes), worst 30 GW; India targets about **40 GW of rooftop by 2030**.
- Storage with RTS: market about **₹1,800 crore (2023)**, mostly **lead-acid**; residential is about 90% of installs with batteries and uses small packs (under 1.5 kWh).
- A solar system **with storage costs 55 to 60% more** (a 4-hour system about 2x). **ROI 3 to 4 years without battery, 6 to 8 years with battery.**
- **Blended cost** (50% storage): about **₹6.9/kWh (1 hour)** to **₹8.9/kWh (4 hours)**, expected to fall below ₹7 by 2032. Report expects solar plus storage to reach **grid parity for most commercial customers by 2026**, already economical in high-tariff states like Maharashtra (commercial tariff ₹15.03/kWh).
- **Policy drivers:**
  - **Draft Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Amendment Rules 2023:** ToD tariffs for C&I consumers above 10 kW from **1 April 2024** and for other consumers (excluding agriculture) from **1 April 2025**; peak tariff at least **1.2x** for C&I (1.1x others); **solar-hour tariff at least 20% lower**.
  - **RPO notification Oct 2023:** new **distributed renewable energy** category, **1.5% in 2024-25 rising to 4.5% by 2029-30** for projects under 10 MW.
  - State incentives for storage with rooftop solar: Goa (50 paise extra for peak supply), Jharkhand, UP, Uttarakhand (₹28,000/kW for BTM up to 1 kW), MP (extra incentives for storage).
  - Standards: BIS certification for batteries, CEA grid connectivity standards (2019).
- Market players: **Exide 34%, Livguard 22%, Luminous 20%**, Okaya 5%, Amara Raja 5%.
- Case study (Amplus HomeScape): 5.4 kW / 9.6 kWh residential system; payback 6.8 to 10.5 years.

### 4. Inverter backup
- Biggest segment (about 20 GWh) but **shrinking about 2% a year** as the grid improves. India had a peak surplus (1,833 MW in 2022-23).
- Market shares: **Exide 29%, Luminous 22%, Livguard 15%**, unorganised 20%. Flooded tubular 45%, flat plate 30%, VRLA 25%, Li-ion under 1%. About 60% of sales are replacements.
- After 28% GST, unorganised share dropped from about 60% (2017-18) to about 20%.

### 5. UPS backup and data centres
- **5 GWh in 2023, about ₹6,000 crore**, growing **6 to 8% CAGR** (base 10 GWh by 2032). **Data centres grow about 10% CAGR**; manufacturing about 8%.
- Li-ion in UPS: about 150 MWh (2023) to about 6.8 GWh (2032) in the base case; **38% CAGR**; hyperscale data centres may reach 80 to 90% adoption. Cost of ownership matters more than upfront cost.
- Barriers: Li-ion above $130/kWh not economical, heat, retrofit issues, no recycling or smelting facilities.
- Leaders: **Amara Raja and Exide hold 71%** of UPS batteries.
- Indian data centre capacity grew from about 540 MW (2019) to over 880 MW (H1 2023); power is about 40% of operating cost.

### 6. Telecom towers
- Adds **40,000 to 50,000 towers** a year. Telecom battery demand grows from about **2.4 GWh to 7.5 GWh (base) by 2032**.
- **Jio put about 2 lakh towers on Li-ion**; Summit Digitel uses Li-ion across its portfolio; Indus is more cautious.
- 5G small cells need smaller batteries (20 to 40 Ah), which partly offsets growth. 5G subscribers: Jio and Airtel hit minimum rollout in all 22 circles in 2023.
- Policy: National Digital Communications Policy 2018, Digital Connectivity Infrastructure Provider licence (TRAI, Aug 2023).

### 7. Railways
- **Indian Railways: net-zero by 2030**, 91% broad-gauge electrified (Sept 2023), 220 MW RE installed with about 3,450 MW in pipeline.
- Storage today: mostly **nickel-cadmium and lead acid** in rolling stock (110 V, 1100 Ah or 120 Ah banks). Demand **2.7 GWh (2023) to 5.1 GWh (2032, base)**; Li-ion starts from 2024 and reaches about 800 MWh by 2032. Opportunities: Vande Bharat trains, wayside storage, regenerative braking, solar plus storage at stations.

### 8. Rural microgrids and decentralised installations
- Includes solar microgrids, solar street lights (UPNEDA tenders gave about 72 MWh of Li-ion in 2023), PM-KUSUM pumps, induction cooking.
- Market is **tiny (about 105 MWh) and shrinking** as grid supply hours improve and government tenders fall.

### 9. Notable BTM projects (2022-23)
- Capgemini Mumbai 3.5 MW BESS and Noida 2.5 MW; Military Engineering Services 1 MW / 2 MWh in Nubra valley; NTPC solar-hydrogen-BESS microgrid in Ladakh; SBI RFP for 13,500 ATMs with UPS; 2.5 MWh Li-ion at a Karnal petrochemical plant to replace diesel generators.

## PART C: Interview Questions and Answers

### FTM report questions

**1. What was the size of India's FTM storage market and what does the report forecast?**
About 220 MWh of BESS and 4.7 GW of pumped hydro installed in 2024. Cumulative 2023-32 potential is about 107 GWh (worst), 173 GWh (base), 255 GWh (best), with utility-scale projects making 93 to 96%.

**2. Why is utility-scale so much larger than discom-side?**
Project size. Utility tenders are hundreds to thousands of MWh each, discom pilots are hundreds of kWh to a few MWh, and several discom projects have been cancelled or delayed.

**3. What are the three scenarios and what drives them?**
Worst: PPA delays and weak policy (about 40% of NEP storage target). Base: business as usual (about 70%). Best: full NEP target and pending policies approved.

**4. What does the National Electricity Plan say about storage needs?**
8.68 GW / 34.72 GWh by 2026-27 and 47.24 GW / 236.22 GWh by 2031-32, plus 26.68 GW pumped storage.

**5. What is the ESO trajectory and what makes it count as fulfilled?**
1% in 2023-24 rising 0.5 points a year to 4% in 2029-30. It counts only if at least 85% of energy stored comes from renewable sources.

**6. What is VGF in the report and how much support does it give?**
VGF of up to 40% of capital cost for standalone BESS under a 4,000 MWh scheme, with commissioning within 24 months of agreement.

**7. What policy gaps does the report highlight?**
No storage-specific policy or clear definition, delayed DSM penalty implementation, weak ToD frameworks, many states without storage in RE policy, no battery reuse and recycling framework, storage not yet in green term-ahead market.

**8. Compare tariffs: FDRE vs peak power vs standalone BESS.**
FDRE tenders cleared at about ₹4.4 to 5.6/kWh depending on the tender. SECI peak power in July 2024 cleared at about ₹3.41 to 3.42/kWh. Standalone BESS is paid a capacity charge, for example GUVNL about ₹3.72 to 4.49 lakh/MW/month. These are not directly comparable because they sell different products.

**9. Why is it hard to compare FDRE tariffs?**
Developers need not disclose their storage size, and the tariff depends on storage size, round-trip efficiency and auxiliary consumption, so you cannot back-calculate the storage from the tariff.

**10. Why did only 480 MW of SECI's 1,500 MW FDRE tender get awarded?**
The report notes undersubscription or bids not meeting requirements; industry also asked for changes like clearer capacities and flexibility for non-working months. Give this as the report's context rather than a firm cause.

**11. Describe the SECI/JSW Fatehgarh BESS pilot.**
500 MW / 1000 MWh at ₹10.835 lakh/MW/month; 150 MW/300 MWh for grid ancillary services (NLDC), 150 MW for the discom, 200 MW merchant; 85% minimum round-trip efficiency, max two cycles/day, 2.5% yearly degradation, 12-year life; no inter-state transmission charges or losses on the ancillary portion.

**12. What is PRAS, SRAS and TRAS and where does storage fit best?**
Primary, secondary and tertiary reserve ancillary services. The report expects most storage demand from secondary reserve; separate storage purely for fast-response ancillary services is unlikely, so stacking with other revenue is needed.

**13. What are the value streams for storage on the discom side?**
Peak shaving, time shifting, DSM penalty savings, power quality, frequency regulation and deferral of grid upgrades (best where afternoon load is low and growth is slow, such as metros).

**14. Why did several discom BESS projects fail or stall?**
The report shows cancellations (BYPL, UPPCL) and projects under review; typical reasons are small scale, unclear regulatory treatment and weak commercial case. Say you would check the individual cases.

**15. What does the MP section of the report say?**
MP Solar Energy Policy 2022: 50% RE mix by 2030, X MW RE needs X/10 MWh storage with 35% CUF for extra incentives, 100% electricity duty exemption for 10 years, stamp duty reimbursement; MP pumped hydro scheme 2023 with a 13 GW tender on hold; MPERC Resource Adequacy Regulations March 2024; Gandhi Sagar 1,440 MW (expanding to 1,920 MW); MPPMCL is a buyer in SECI's FDRE tender.

**16. How does MP compare with other states in the report's table?**
MP has storage targets, special tariff, incentives, duty exemption, R&D support and ESO, but no transmission charge exemption and no must-run status. Rajasthan scores yes on almost every column.

**17. What is the pumped hydro situation?**
Large pipeline but about 3.3 GW operational of about 65 GW identified; most under survey. Off-stream, closed-loop projects are faster; abandoned mines are an idea; Gandhi Sagar is a flagship.

**18. What are open-loop and closed-loop pumped storage?**
Open-loop is linked to a natural water body and needs heavy environmental clearance. Closed-loop uses artificial reservoirs and is more flexible, so it is increasingly preferred.

**19. What is blended cost and what assumptions drive it?**
It is the LCOE of renewables plus storage. The report assumes LFP, storage at 50% of project capacity, 70:30 debt-equity, and delivery of at least 50% of installed capacity for 6 lean hours a day. More storage hours improves availability but raises the tariff.

**20. What is the lesson from the Rajasthan case study?**
With about 58 GW solar by 2030 the state needs up to 31 GWh of storage, split between short-duration batteries and long-duration pumped hydro.

**21. Name the key developers winning storage tenders.**
ACME, ReNew, Juniper Green, Hero Future, Tata Power, Serentica, JSW Neo Energy, IndiGrid, Gensol, Reliance Power.

**22. Why is DSM penalty reduction part of the storage case?**
Discoms and RE generators pay when they deviate from schedule. A battery smooths output and demand, so penalties fall. The report also notes DSM penalty enforcement is delayed in some states.

**23. What does the Tata Power Chhattisgarh project show?**
100 MW solar with 40 MW / 120 MWh BESS, for peak-hour supply to CSPDCL, commissioned March 2024; shows co-located solar plus storage working at scale in India.

**24. What does the sodium-ion case study in China show?**
A 100 MWh sodium-ion station (Datang Hubei, July 2024) shows an alternative chemistry that reduces dependence on lithium.

**25. What would you check before trusting these forecasts?**
Date of the data (Sept 2024), which tenders were actually commissioned, changes in VGF and ESO, battery price movements, and PPA signing rates.

### BTM report questions

**26. How big is the BTM storage market and where is it going?**
33 GWh in 2023, base case about 44 GWh by 2032 (3% CAGR). Growth comes from telecom, data centres/UPS, rooftop solar and railways; inverters shrink.

**27. Why is the inverter market shrinking?**
Grid reliability has improved; the market is falling about 2% a year from about 20 GWh. Most sales are replacements, and consumers are moving to higher-capacity inverters with shorter backup.

**28. Why is Li-ion penetration so high in telecom but low elsewhere?**
Tower companies buy in bulk and value low maintenance, high depth of discharge and long life; Jio's roughly 2 lakh Li-ion towers and Summit Digitel led. Other segments are price-sensitive and lead-acid is much cheaper.

**29. What drives UPS battery growth?**
Data centres (about 10% CAGR), manufacturing (about 8%), healthcare and smart buildings. Total cost of ownership, space and weight favour Li-ion, but price above $130/kWh and lack of recycling are barriers.

**30. What are the economics of rooftop solar with storage?**
Solar plus storage costs 55 to 60% more; payback goes from 3 to 4 years to 6 to 8 years. Blended cost about ₹6.9 to 8.9/kWh vs commercial tariffs of about ₹8 to 15/kWh, so it already works in high-tariff states.

**31. How do the 2023 ToD rules help BTM storage?**
Peak tariffs at least 1.2x for C&I and solar-hour tariffs at least 20% lower, from April 2024 for C&I above 10 kW and April 2025 for others. This creates a price spread that a battery can exploit by shifting.

**32. What is the distributed renewable energy obligation?**
From the Oct 2023 RPO notification: discoms must buy at least 1.5% from distributed RE (projects under 10 MW) in 2024-25, rising to 4.5% by 2029-30.

**33. What is the trend in battery pack prices?**
LFP about $156/kWh in 2023 falling to about $94/kWh by 2032; NMC from about $200 to $120. Cells are about 70% of pack cost and are imported.

**34. Why does GST matter?**
Lead-acid carries 28% and Li-ion 18%; after the 28% GST many unorganised lead-acid players exited, so organised players (Exide, Luminous, Livguard) now hold about 80% of the inverter market.

**35. Who leads inverter, rooftop solar and UPS batteries?**
Inverter: Exide 29%, Luminous 22%, Livguard 15%. Rooftop solar batteries: Exide 34%, Livguard 22%, Luminous 20%. UPS: Amara Raja and Exide 71%.

**36. What role can storage play on Indian Railways?**
Rolling stock batteries (nickel-cadmium, lead acid) will gradually shift to Li-ion; wayside storage, regenerative braking and solar plus storage support the 2030 net-zero goal. Demand grows from 2.7 to 5.1 GWh by 2032.

**37. Why are microgrids and street lights shrinking?**
Improving grid supply hours and fewer government tenders; this segment is only about 105 MWh.

**38. What risks does the BTM report flag?**
Improving grid reliability reducing backup need, low C&I willingness to invest in storage with rooftop solar, lean 5G sites needing smaller batteries, lithium price instability, and lack of recycling infrastructure.

**39. What are the best, base and worst cases for BTM?**
Best: 40 GW rooftop target met, faster 5G and C&I growth, subsidies (4% CAGR, 51 GWh). Base: business as usual (3%, 44 GWh). Worst: reliability improves, weak C&I and telecom demand (1%, 37 GWh).

**40. How are FTM and BTM different in this pair of reports?**
FTM is policy- and tender-driven, growing fast from a tiny base (220 MWh to 100+ GWh). BTM is already large (33 GWh) but mostly lead-acid, reliability-driven and growing slowly.

### Cross-report and MP-linked questions

**41. Which is bigger today and which is bigger by 2032, FTM or BTM?**
BTM is far bigger today (about 33 GWh of annual demand in 2023 vs 0.22 GWh of installed FTM BESS). By 2032 the base-case FTM annual additions (about 42 GWh) roughly match BTM annual demand (about 44 GWh), so FTM catches up even though its cumulative total is only about 173 GWh.

**42. What would you advise an MP developer based on both reports?**
For FTM, bid for MPPMCL's standalone BESS with VGF and consider co-located solar plus storage under MP's X/10 MWh incentive; for BTM, target high-tariff C&I and data centre customers using ToD arbitrage.

**43. What questions would you ask the client before sizing a BESS?**
Load profile, tariff structure and ToD bands, connection voltage, available land, offtake contract, VGF eligibility, round-trip efficiency requirement, cycles per day, degradation guarantees.

**44. How would you update this analysis for 2026?**
Check new tenders (MP's 750 MW and 625 MW BESS), the 2025 VGF round, battery price falls, ToD and smart meter rollout, ESO enforcement, and updated NEP and CEA numbers.

**45. What numbers must you remember from each report?**
FTM: 220 MWh BESS, 4.7 GW PHES, 173 GWh base-case cumulative by 2032, ESO 1% to 4%, VGF up to 40%, NEP 236 GWh by 2031-32. BTM: 33 GWh in 2023, 44 GWh by 2032, inverters 20 GWh shrinking 2% a year, LFP $156 to $94/kWh, Li-ion penetration telecom 67%.

## Caveats
- I extracted text and chart data from the slides; some slides were mostly images (logos, supplier maps, diagrams), so those details are not covered.
- A few numbers in the reports look inconsistent or typo-prone (for example the wind slide says "installed solar capacity", and some CAGR and tariff units look garbled). Verify any figure you plan to quote against the original slide.
- Anything marked "report's context" in Q10 and Q14 is my inference, not stated by the report.
